\chapter{Introduction}

\section{Introduction to Durable Execution}

A substantial and growing fraction of modern software is not a request that is
answered in milliseconds, but a \emph{process} that unfolds over time. Placing
a food order charges a card, reserves stock, notifies a restaurant and
dispatches a driver. Settling an invoice debits one ledger, credits another,
issues a receipt and files a tax record. Provisioning a cluster allocates
machines, attaches storage, installs software and registers health checks. An
artificial-intelligence agent asked to arrange travel plans an itinerary,
calls a booking tool, waits for a human to approve the result and then
confirms it. These are all instances of the same computational shape: a
multi-step process, spanning several independent services, whose steps have
consequences in the real world, and whose total duration is measured not in
milliseconds but in seconds, hours or days.

Such a process is called a \emph{workflow}. The defining difficulty of a
workflow is not its length but the combination of two properties. First, it
is \emph{stateful}: step four is meaningful only in the context of what steps
one through three decided. Second, its steps produce \emph{external side
effects} that cannot be withdrawn by the system that caused them. Once a
payment gateway has moved money, no amount of local rollback will unmove it.
A database transaction has neither problem, because it is short enough to hold
locks for its whole life and because its effects are confined to the database
that can also undo them. A workflow has both.

The consequence is that an ordinary crash --- a process being killed, a
machine losing power, a container being rescheduled --- becomes a correctness
problem rather than an availability problem. Consider the order workflow
interrupted immediately after the card has been charged and the stock
reserved, but before the restaurant has been notified. A system that has kept
no durable record of its own progress has exactly two options when it restarts,
and Fig.~\ref{fig:crashdilemma} shows that both of them are wrong. It may
begin the process again from the first step, in which case the card is charged
a second time and the stock is reserved twice. Or it may abandon the process,
in which case the customer's money has been taken, inventory is held against
an order that will never be fulfilled, and no food is ever delivered. The
first outcome duplicates work; the second loses it. There is no third option
available to a system that does not remember what it had already done.

The practical responses to this problem in ordinary engineering practice are
ad-hoc retry loops, cron jobs that poll a status column, and message queues
whose consumers track progress in hand-written application tables. Each of
these works for a particular workflow and fails for the next one, because the
recovery logic is written once per process rather than once per system. A
2026 study of data-oriented workflow systems quantified the resulting burden
directly, observing that in such applications ``the forward logic is 10\% of
the application while the error and oops logic are 90\%''~\cite{acdc2026}.
Nine-tenths of the code exists to handle the ways things go wrong.

\emph{Durable execution} is the name given to the class of systems that solve
this problem once, generically, for every workflow written on top of them. The
central idea is deceptively simple, and is stated here because the whole of
this report follows from it:

\begin{quote}
\textbf{Every decision a workflow makes is written to an append-only log
before it is acted upon, and recovery is performed by re-executing the
workflow's own code against that log.}
\end{quote}

Because the log is written first, nothing that has happened in the outside
world can be absent from the record for long. Because recovery is a
re-execution rather than a restoration of saved memory, the engine does not
need to snapshot stacks, threads or heaps: it replays the function, the
completed steps return their recorded results instead of running again, and
the program counter arrives naturally at the point the failed machine had
reached. A workflow interrupted on one machine can therefore resume on a
completely different machine, because nothing machine-local was ever part of
its state.

The industry has converged on this approach. Uber's Cadence executes on the
order of twelve billion task runs per month for rides and food delivery;
Netflix's Conductor drives video encoding pipelines that run for days; Temporal
has become the de-facto commercial standard; and Restate offers a more recent,
log-native formulation of the same idea. The academic lineage is equally
clear: the DBOS line of work from Stanford and MIT has argued since 2022 that
durable execution belongs inside the database rather than outside
it~\cite{dbos2022, dbos2025}, and R3 gave a formal treatment of
record-and-replay for database-backed applications~\cite{r3vldb2023}.

What is far less well established is how such an engine is built. The internal
mechanisms --- how the log is partitioned, how a workflow's state is
reconstructed deterministically, how ownership of a partition moves safely
between machines when one of them dies --- are rarely implemented from first
principles, even in distributed-systems coursework that covers replication,
partitioning and consensus in the abstract. This project closes that gap by
designing and building such an engine rather than by using one.

\section{Motivation}

Three observations motivate this work.

\subsection{Existing engines require a great deal of infrastructure}

Every production durable execution system surveyed for this project requires
between four and six cooperating infrastructure components. Netflix's
Conductor needs a database, an Elasticsearch cluster, a message queue, a
central server and a worker fleet. Apache Airflow needs PostgreSQL, Redis or
RabbitMQ, a scheduler process and Celery workers. Meta's Shard Manager, which
partitions work across roughly a million machines, still requires ZooKeeper as
a separate coordination service~\cite{shardmanager2021}.
Fig.~\ref{fig:depcount} puts these counts side by side.

Each additional component is an independent failure domain, an additional
thing to configure, monitor, upgrade and reason about during an incident. For
a small deployment the operational cost of the orchestration system can easily
exceed the cost of the application it orchestrates. The question this project
asks is whether the guarantees can be preserved while the dependency count is
reduced to one.

\subsection{Horizontal partitioning and operational simplicity are usually
  traded against one another}

The systems that are simple are not partitioned, and the systems that are
partitioned are not simple. DBOS and the AC/DC work that builds on it obtain
durable execution with very few moving parts, but run on a single
node~\cite{dbos2025, acdc2026}. Temporal, Cadence and Shard Manager partition
execution across machines, but reintroduce either a dedicated coordination
cluster or a multi-service control plane in order to decide who owns what.
This project takes the position that the two properties are not in fact in
tension, because a relational database already provides the linearizable
compare-and-swap that partition ownership requires. What is needed is not a
consensus cluster but a correctly designed use of one database row --- a claim
developed in detail in Section~4.1.5.

\subsection{Agent-driven workflows break the assumption behind deduplication}

The third motivation is newer, and is the research contribution of this work.
Every durable execution engine prevents duplicate side effects by recognising
a repeated request. Every one of them recognises it by comparing the request's
\emph{bytes} --- a hash, an idempotency key, an exact match on recorded
arguments. That technique is sound when the arguments are frozen at the moment
the step was first scheduled, which is exactly the case for an engine-driven
retry.

It is not sound when the caller is a large-language-model agent. When such an
agent crashes and recovers, it does not replay a frozen argument list: it
\emph{re-plans}. A planning step that runs again at a non-zero sampling
temperature produces a semantically equivalent instruction expressed in
different words. An agent that had decided to book a flight described as
``BLR$\rightarrow$DEL Friday morning'' may, after recovery, re-issue the same
intent as ``Bangalore to Delhi, Friday AM''. The two strings hash differently,
every exact-match check passes them as distinct, and the flight is booked
twice. This failure mode is not hypothetical; large-scale empirical studies of
multi-agent system failures identify step repetition as a recurring, measured
category~\cite{whyfail2025, wherefail2025}, and the Microsoft AI Red Team's
taxonomy of agentic failure modes documents the same
class~\cite{msredteam2025}. Chapter~2 develops the point in full, and
Section~4.2.10 gives the design that addresses it.

\section{Problem Statement}

The problem addressed by this project is stated as follows.

\begin{quote}
To design and implement a horizontally-sharded, replicated durable execution
engine that guarantees long-running, multi-step workflows execute reliably to
completion --- surviving worker crashes, node failures and partial failures ---
through event sourcing, deterministic replay, idempotent activity execution and
lease-based fault tolerance, deployable across a small multi-node cluster
without requiring specialised or large-scale infrastructure; and to extend its
deduplication mechanism so that semantically equivalent but textually different
repeated actions, of the kind produced by a recovering AI agent, are detected
before the corresponding side effect is performed a second time.
\end{quote}

Two parts of that statement deserve emphasis because they bound the work
honestly. The engine targets \emph{correctness and fault tolerance at a
moderate, demonstrable scale} rather than hyperscale throughput, which is
outside the reach of a single-developer academic project. And ownership
consensus is obtained through a lease-renewal mechanism backed by a
linearizable database, not through a full Raft implementation, which is noted
in Section~1.5 as a limitation and a possible extension.

\section{Scope and Limitations}

The scope of this project is the engine itself and two demonstration
applications built on it. Stating the limitations plainly at the outset is
deliberate: several of them are consequences of design decisions defended in
Chapter~4, and a reader who knows them in advance will read those defences
more critically.

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*, itemsep=2pt]
  \item \textbf{This is at-least-once execution with strong deduplication, not
    exactly-once execution.} A crash occurring after a side effect has been
    performed but before it has been recorded is indistinguishable, from the
    engine's point of view, from a crash occurring before the side effect. No
    system that crosses a process boundary can eliminate this window. The
    engine's contribution is to generate a stable deduplication key that the
    downstream resource can use to close it. This is analysed in
    Section~4.2.8.
  \item \textbf{Shards share one database instance}, separated by a shard
    identifier column, rather than each having a database of its own. Every key
    already carries the shard identifier, so a per-database deployment is a
    change of configuration rather than of schema, but it has not been built.
  \item \textbf{Ownership is not consensus.} The design relies on PostgreSQL as
    a single linearizable source of truth for the ownership record. This is a
    legitimate and deliberate position, and it is the same one DBOS takes, but
    it moves the coordination problem into the database rather than eliminating
    it, and it makes the database the availability floor.
  \item \textbf{The event log has no garbage collection} and grows without
    bound. Retention and archival are future work.
  \item \textbf{Semantic idempotency is opt-in and conservative.} Its failure
    modes are asymmetric: a false negative merely matches the exact-match
    baseline, whereas a false positive silently suppresses an action the user
    genuinely wanted. The design therefore defaults to exact matching, requires
    the semantic mode to be requested per activity, and journals every verdict
    for audit. Section~4.2.10 treats this asymmetry as a designed constraint
    rather than a defect to be hidden.
  \item \textbf{Measurement is performed on a single development host} running
    containers, not on a data-centre cluster. Absolute throughput measured in
    this environment is therefore not comparable with published figures from
    production systems. What is comparable, and what this project reports, is
    the \emph{shape} of scaling behaviour and the \emph{counts} of lost and
    duplicated work, neither of which depends on hardware.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Contributions}

The contributions of this work are the following.

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*, itemsep=2pt]
  \item \textbf{A durable execution engine with exactly one infrastructure
    dependency.} Event sourcing, deterministic replay, a durable task queue,
    durable timers, signals, sagas and shard failover are all implemented over
    PostgreSQL alone, with no message broker, no coordination service and no
    consensus cluster.
  \item \textbf{Persistence-layer fencing.} Shard ownership safety is obtained
    by re-verifying a monotonic fencing token inside the same transaction as
    every write, using \cd{SELECT \ldots{} FOR UPDATE} to serialise the check
    against the write. This yields the property normally supplied by ZooKeeper
    --- a node that has lost ownership cannot mutate shard state regardless of
    what it believes --- without a second cluster to operate.
  \item \textbf{A two-level mapping that makes rebalancing an ownership
    handoff.} Workflows are hashed to a fixed number of shards and shards are
    assigned to nodes by a consistent hash ring. Because the first mapping never
    changes, adding a node moves ownership of a minority of shards and migrates
    no data at all.
  \item \textbf{Semantic idempotency as a journalled decision.} The engine
    compares the \emph{meaning} of an action an agent is about to take against
    the actions already in the workflow's own history, and suppresses the
    duplicate before the side effect occurs. The non-deterministic verdict is
    computed once and written to the log, so replay is deterministic, requires
    no network access, and every suppression is auditable from a row. To the
    authors' knowledge no existing durable execution engine inspects workflow
    history in this way.
  \item \textbf{An explicit account of what the design does not achieve},
    including the irreducible crash window, the asymmetric false-positive
    behaviour of the semantic check, and a database failover time that is
    worse than published work.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Organisation of the Report}

The remainder of this report is organised as follows.
\textbf{Chapter~2} surveys the 2020--2026 literature across the six areas this
work touches --- durable execution, partition management, ownership and
coordination, compensation, agent failure modes and semantic similarity ---
and identifies the research gap precisely.
\textbf{Chapter~3} derives the functional and non-functional requirements,
specifies the software and hardware the system needs, and records the
assumptions and constraints under which it was built and measured.
\textbf{Chapter~4} presents the system design: Section~4.1 gives the
high-level architecture, the principles it follows and the alternatives
rejected, and Section~4.2 gives the low-level design --- the data model, the
execution loop, the concurrency mechanism, the failure analysis and the
semantic deduplication decision path.
\textbf{Chapters~5 through~8} cover implementation, testing, results and
conclusions, and are the subject of the subsequent phase of the project.
